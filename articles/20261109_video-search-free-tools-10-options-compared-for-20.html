Video Search Free Tools: 10 Options Compared for 2026

A safety engineer has 400 hours of warehouse robot footage and needs the three minutes where a pallet fell. A dashcam fleet manager needs every clip with a white van at night. An editor cutting an advertisement needs one spoken line buried in a day of GoPro takes. All three start with a tool for Video Search free of charge, and all three hit the same wall quickly.

The footage isn't named for what happened in it. Nobody tagged the pallet, the van, or the line when it was recorded, so the only fallback is scrubbing timelines by hand. That's the scrubbing problem these tools are supposed to solve, and it's why where the search runs matters more than the demo. Local desktop apps keep CCTV and workplace video off the cloud. Browser tools trade privacy for convenience and tight quotas. Public web search won't touch your private files at all. SDKs put search inside a mobile app, smart glasses, or a robot, but you have to build the product around them.

  • Unlabeled continuous footage: CCTV, dashcams, and robots record for hours with no useful filenames or tags.
  • Split evidence: The decisive clue may be a sound, a spoken command, or text in the scene, not a visible object.
  • Bandwidth and privacy: Uploading sensitive footage is slow, costly, and sometimes not allowed.

I sorted ten free or freemium options by those limits, not by marketing claims.

1. V-Modal AI

V-Modal AI on DevHunt is a search layer for Physical AI, built for developers adding visual retrieval to a product rather than for people seeking a finished desktop app. Users can search video and image data with a text query or an input image. Video search analyzes frames and context, so results can point to a moment by meaning without manual tags or timeline scrubbing.

Where It Fits

The SDK offerings are native Android in Kotlin and Flutter. Mobile media tools include chunked multipart uploads, signed streaming URLs, progress reporting, and upload cancellation. These features help developers manage large media transfers over mobile or edge connections. To get started, add the SDK to an app, configure a project with an API key, upload a video, and search for a moment. The source says this takes minutes.

For media libraries and mobile apps, viewers can jump to a precise scene. E-commerce apps can surface product clips from an image or description. CCTV workflows can search hours of footage for a specific visual indicator. The same visual search capabilities are designed for mobile, edge devices, smart glasses, and robotics. Smart glasses can query a visual memory of what they encounter.

My judgement: Choose V-Modal AI when you need to build search into an app or device. Choose a desktop tool for ready-to-use local searching.

Pricing details are not included here. Check the API access and fit for your footage before committing. The Android SDK, com.vmodal.vmodal-sdk-android, is published in the V-Modal AI Android SDK repository, which is licensed under Apache-2.0.

2. frameseek

frameseek is a free desktop app for searching a personal library with natural-language descriptions. You describe a moment in plain words and it returns matching timestamps you can export as clips. It handles MP4, MOV, and MKV among other common formats, so GoPro and phone footage drop in without conversion.

The headline feature is that it works offline on Windows and macOS. For home CCTV, family archives, or workplace recordings you can't upload, that's the whole decision. There's no sign-in and no credit card, which makes it the lowest-friction way to test whether semantic search helps your library at all.

  • Scene detection: Automatic grouping saves you hunting through one long continuous file.
  • Frame-level timestamps: Results point at moments, not whole files.
  • Clip export: Pull the matched segment straight into an edit.

The limits are plain. It's desktop only, so there's no mobile or edge story for smart glasses or robots. Published features emphasize visual analysis of objects, actions, text, and context rather than audio or transcript search, so a spoken phrase with nothing distinctive on screen is a weak query here. If you're tracking how tools like this surface in developer circles, Trendshift's trending repositories is a useful signal to watch alongside download pages.

3. FrameSeek

The browser-based FrameSeek is a different product from the desktop app. It combines visual scene search with automatic speech transcription, letting you search a library for what appears on screen or what someone says.

For ad-edit workflows, that combination can save time. Search for the product name in the transcript, then use visual search to find takes where the presenter holds the box. Shot grouping, folders, and filters help organize the results. You can jump to a moment and export an MP4 clip without installing software.

The Free Tier Wall

The free plan has tight storage, a monthly limit on visual searches, and a per-video size limit. A single afternoon of 4K dashcam or GoPro footage may exceed those limits. Sign-in is required, and footage is processed off your machine.

Use it to evaluate short, speech-heavy clips that you are comfortable uploading. It is a poor fit for continuous CCTV, private surveillance archives, or libraries measured in hundreds of hours. For a quick product check, FrameSeek's repository activity offers another signal alongside the service itself.

4. FrameQuery

FrameQuery splits the job in two, and you need to understand the split before you call it free. Searching an existing index is free. Creating that index with cloud processing is paid. If your footage is already indexed, ongoing search costs you nothing. If it isn't, budget for the indexing step.

Once indexed, you can search across transcripts, objects, and scene descriptions, plus ordinary metadata. The index itself stays on your machine, with backup options for local storage and cloud drives. That means no ongoing storage charge for the index and no dependency on the vendor keeping your library online.

An index you own locally keeps working even if you stop paying for new processing.

That model fits a fixed archive well. Think of a training library, a finished documentary project, or a set of product demonstration videos that won't grow much. You pay once to make it searchable, then search it for free.

It fits a growing surveillance or robotics feed badly. Every new hour needs paid cloud indexing first, so costs scale with recording time. And because indexing depends on cloud compute, it's not a fit if footage must never leave the device, no matter where the finished index lives.

5. Moment Search

Moment Search is a desktop app that keeps processing on your own machine. It offers visual search and speech search together, with Whisper transcription named among its tools, and exports clips without cloud uploads. For interviews, lectures, or meeting recordings, the speech side is what separates it from purely visual tools.

There's no account required to download and a free starter tier to test with. Local processing is a genuine advantage for dashcam footage with passengers talking, or workplace video with people in frame. Nothing needs to be sent anywhere for a result.

The free tier is a trial in size, not just in name. You're limited to one footage source and 25 indexed videos, which is enough to judge result quality and not much more. Supported systems are macOS 11 or later on Apple Silicon and Windows 10 or later, and the product is still in beta, so expect rough edges. Developers comparing local-first approaches often start by reading what's public on GitHub before trusting a beta with a large archive.

6. Splayed

Splayed searches more than video. It covers video, audio, images, PDFs, and text on your own computer, and within video it can search footage, dialogue, sound, and scene descriptions. Queries can be text, an image, or a video clip, which helps when you have an example frame but no good words for it.

The site says it's free with no quotas and that media stays on your computer. Watched folders keep a working library indexed as new files arrive. For advertisement video edition and broader media content work, the described workflows with Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve, and MCP-compatible AI tools are the draw, because search results land where the edit already happens.

  • Multimodal Audio: Dialogue and sound are searchable, not just frames.
  • Image queries: Find similar shots across a shoot without naming them.
  • Local first: No upload queue for client footage under embargo.

The catch is scope and maturity. It's Mac focused, initial indexing consumes your own processing time and storage, and long-term support is hard to judge from public information. Test it on one project folder before pointing it at your whole archive.

7. Google Videos

Google Search is free Video Search for the public web, not for your files. The Videos tab surfaces publicly available clips from ordinary queries, and some results include Key Moments that jump to a section inside a video. When those appear, they save real time on tutorials and talks.

Scale explains why users expect this to work everywhere. By 2024, approximately 2.5 billion people, about 32% of the global population, watched YouTube, according to Exploding Topics' SEO statistics roundup. The same source notes estimates of more than 500 hours of new video uploaded every minute. People learned to search video with short natural-language queries because Google and YouTube trained them to.

That expectation breaks on private footage. Google doesn't search your local files, results are limited, and Key Moments show up only on some results. It's excellent for finding a public explainer or checking what competitors published. It does nothing for CCTV review, dashcam incidents, or a robot's own recordings, which is why developer discussions on Dev.to about private library search quickly move to local tools and SDKs.

8. Bing Video Search

Bing Video Search is Microsoft's free public-web equivalent. You search by keywords or categories and browse popular, recently uploaded, and related videos. Microsoft describes it as aggregating results from multiple sources, including YouTube and Vimeo, which makes it a useful second index when Google's ranking buries what you need.

For media content research, running the same query on both engines is cheap and sometimes decisive. A product clip, a public safety video, or an older upload on a smaller platform may surface in one index and not the other. Category browsing also helps when you don't yet have precise keywords for a topic.

What It Cannot Do

Everything here is limited to what Bing has indexed publicly. There's no personal library search, no frame-level natural-language search over your own recordings, and no audio search across your surveillance archive. Ranking and availability depend entirely on Bing's index, not on your footage.

Use it for discovery and cross-checking public sources. Don't plan a dashcam, CCTV, or smart-glasses workflow around it. If the video isn't published on the open web, this tool cannot see it.

9. InVID-WeVerify Verification Plugin

The InVID-WeVerify Verification Plugin answers a different question. Not where is the moment in my library, but is this clip what it claims to be. It's a free browser plugin built for journalists and fact-checkers verifying online video.

It extracts keyframes from supported video URLs or uploaded files so each frame can be checked with reverse-image search engines, and it adds context lookups plus metadata and image-forensics tools. Say a clip claims to show a factory incident this week. Keyframes may reveal the same scene published years earlier somewhere else. That's verification work no semantic library search does for you.

Its limits follow from that purpose. It doesn't perform semantic search across a personal video library, and results depend on external image-search engines and public indexing. An unpublished clip returns little. The official page lists an older plugin release, so check browser compatibility before you rely on it during a breaking-news check.

For surveillance and media teams handling third-party footage, it's a specialist worth keeping installed. It complements library search rather than replacing it.

10. Reverse Video Search

Reverse Video Search takes the keyframe idea and strips it to a simple browser workflow. You upload a video or paste a URL, it extracts keyframes, and you run those frames through supported reverse-image services yourself. The manual workflow is free and, as described on the site, needs no account.

This is the practical tool for tracing reposts. Media and advertising teams use it to find where a clip first appeared, spot uncredited reuse of their own content, or check whether user-submitted footage is original. It works from desktop and mobile browsers, so a quick source check doesn't need a workstation. Community reports on Reddit are often where users compare which frames returned hits.

Be clear about what you're searching. It matches frames, not meaning, speech, or sound, so success depends on distinctive frames and public indexing. A generic warehouse interior or a night road scene may return nothing useful. The optional DeepSearch automates broader searching, but it costs extra, so the free tier is manual work in exchange for zero spend.

Match the Free Tool to Where Your Footage Lives

Start with the footage's location and the work it needs to support. If files must stay on the device, test a local app. If you need to verify public clips, use keyframes or reverse-image search; semantic search across a private library will not identify a clip's original source. If you are adding search to a mobile app, smart glasses, dashcam platform, or robot, look for an SDK and test it with your own file sizes and network conditions.

Archive growth makes the deployment choice matter. TheStacc's video marketing statistics places video at roughly 80% to 82% of overall internet traffic and says 45% of internet users watch at least one hour of online video each day. Choose a setup you can maintain as the archive grows, including its storage, indexing, and network demands.

Search quality has limits. In the Video-BrowseComp benchmark paper, GPT-5.1 with search reached 15.24% accuracy on 210 open-web questions requiring navigation of video timelines. Simple object and keyword queries are more straightforward. Questions about a sequence of events over time remain difficult, so check the timestamps rather than trusting a promising result preview.

Test rule: Run one hour of representative footage, ask twenty real questions, and count how often the first result lands on the right timestamp.

For Physical AI, match search to the device and its data. A 2025 market assessment in GlobeNewswire's Physical AI release reported computer vision at 45.02% of the Physical AI market and industrial robots at 36.12%. Robots and other devices that capture visual data throughout operation need a way to retrieve specific moments, not just store footage.

Robotics footage may carry more than images. IBM's robotics data gap article describes the RH20T dataset as containing more than 110,000 robot sequences across approximately 150 tasks. When a sequence includes force, torque, audio, and tactile signals, a visual-only search can miss useful evidence. A gripper contact, a change in motor sound, and a spoken stop command may describe the same incident.

Smart glasses face a related retrieval problem. The ACM digital library piece reports Meta's smart glasses reached 2 million units by February 2025. Devices that capture continuously operate within battery and bandwidth limits. Searching an entire day by hand is a poor fit.
